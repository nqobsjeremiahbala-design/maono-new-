The gap between a mediocre trader and a consistent one is rarely about strategy. It is about self-awareness.

A trading journal is the cheapest tool available for closing that gap. It costs nothing. It takes ten minutes a day. It is also the habit most traders refuse to keep, which is probably why most traders stay where they are.

## What a journal is not

A journal is not a spreadsheet of trades with a PnL column. That is a track record. Useful, but different.

A journal is a written record of **what you thought, what you did, and why**. The PnL is the smallest part of it. The reasoning is the whole point.

## What to write

Before the trade:

- What is the setup I am seeing?
- What is my entry, stop, and target?
- What is the size of my risk in currency terms?
- How do I feel right now? Calm, anxious, desperate to trade?

After the trade:

- Did I follow my plan?
- If I broke a rule, which one and why?
- What was happening in my head during the trade?
- What is one sentence of honest feedback to myself?

The pre-trade section catches impulsive trades. Forcing yourself to write the reason slows you down enough that half of your worst trades will not get taken.

The post-trade section catches patterns. Over a month, you will see that you break the same rule in the same emotional state. That is actionable. You cannot fix a problem you have not named.

## The weekly review

On Sunday, read through the week's entries. Look for patterns, not outcomes.

Questions to ask:

- Which trades this week did I take outside my plan?
- What emotion was I feeling when I took them?
- Were my winning trades my best trades, or lucky ones?
- Were my losing trades mistakes, or just normal variance?

The distinction between a bad trade and a bad outcome is everything. A trade that followed the plan and lost is fine. A trade that broke the plan and won is dangerous, because it teaches you the wrong lesson.

## Why it works

Writing slows you down. That is the mechanism. Trading is mostly a speed game where your brain wants to act faster than your discipline allows. Journaling introduces friction at the exact moment you need it.

Over time, the journal becomes a mirror. You see who you actually are as a trader, not who you imagined you were. That is uncomfortable, which is why most traders stop after two weeks.

The ones who do not stop are the ones who end up consistent.

## Start smaller than you think

Do not design a perfect journal template. Do not buy software. Open a Google Doc and write one paragraph about every trade you take this week. That is enough to start.

If you cannot commit ten minutes a day to understanding how you trade, the honest conclusion is that trading is not for you yet. That is okay. The journal will tell you that too.

And that is worth more than the next indicator.
