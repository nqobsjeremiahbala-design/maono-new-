Every trading book tells you to write a trading plan. Most traders write a two-page document, read it once, and never look at it again.

The problem is not that traders are lazy. The problem is that most plans are written for a version of the trader that does not exist, the one who reads their plan before every trade and follows it calmly at 03:00 during a news spike.

This is a shorter, more honest framework. A plan that fits on one page and survives contact with a bad Monday morning.

## Part one: when you trade

Before you think about setups, answer three questions:

- What sessions will you trade? London, New York, the overlap, or the Asian range?
- What days will you trade? Some traders skip Mondays and Fridays because of the weekend gap and Friday volatility.
- What news events make you stand aside? NFP, CPI, central bank rate decisions are the usual list.

Decide this once. Write it down. Stick to it. Most undisciplined trades come from trading outside the hours you perform best in.

## Part two: what you trade

Pick your pairs. Most traders would be better off specialising in two or three pairs than skimming through twelve. You learn the personality of a pair by trading it, not by reading about it.

Decide on your timeframes. A clean setup needs a higher timeframe (4H or daily) for context and a lower timeframe (15m or 1H) for entry. If you are always flipping between 1m, 5m, and 4H, you are not executing, you are searching.

## Part three: the setup, in plain language

Write your A+ setup in a single paragraph that a friend could read and understand.

Something like: *"I take a trade when price breaks a higher timeframe level with momentum, retraces into the prior structure, and shows rejection on the 15-minute. I enter on the first valid candle close, stop goes above the recent swing, target is the next major level."*

That is a plan. Not a flowchart. Not a 50-page PDF. A paragraph.

If you cannot write your setup in one paragraph, you do not have a setup. You have a vibe.

## Part four: risk and management

Three numbers. Write them down.

1. Max risk per trade, as a percentage (1 percent or less).
2. Max open risk at any one time (usually 2 to 3 percent across all trades).
3. Daily loss limit that ends your session (often 2 to 3 percent).

When the daily limit is hit, you stop. Close the platform. The point is not punishment. The point is that after two losses in a row, your judgement is already worse than it was an hour ago. Stopping protects tomorrow.

## Part five: the review

Every Sunday, take ten minutes:

- Did I follow my plan this week?
- Which trades broke the rules?
- What was the emotional state behind those trades?
- What is one thing to change next week?

The review is the plan. Writing a plan and not reviewing it is like making a budget and never checking your bank account.

## Keep it one page

Your entire plan should fit on one printed page. If it is longer, it is a manual, and no one reads the manual.

The goal is not to write a beautiful plan. The goal is to build a set of rules you are actually willing to follow at 07:00 on a Monday after a losing week.

Start short. Iterate.
